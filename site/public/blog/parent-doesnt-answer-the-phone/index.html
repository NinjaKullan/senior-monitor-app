What to do when a parent doesn't answer the phone

Hema · Founder, HeyKettle

August 30, 2026 · A guide, not a letter

My mother is hard of hearing, and when she doesn't have her hearing aid in, the phone can ring itself out on the table beside her. So I've had a lot of practice at this particular hour, the one where the call doesn't connect and you start doing arithmetic about time zones and last messages. Almost every time, it has been nothing.

The trouble is that "almost every time" is not a plan, and the reason this hour is so unpleasant is that most of us are improvising it while our heart rate climbs. So here's the order of operations I wish I'd had written down years ago. None of it is dramatic. That's the point.

Start with the boring explanations, because they're usually right

Before anything else, run through the everyday reasons, and be honest about which of them apply to your parent specifically. The phone is dead or was never charged. The ringer is off, or got turned down by accident and nobody noticed. The hearing aid is out. The phone is in the bedroom and they are in the garden. They are asleep, in the shower, at the temple, at the market, at a neighbor's. They saw it ring and decided to call you back after the show finished.

If your parent has a pattern of any of these, that pattern is information. Someone whose phone is always dead by four in the afternoon is not sending you a signal by not answering at five.

The first hour

Call again after twenty minutes or so rather than eight times in a row. A line that rings out repeatedly does nothing except make the voicemail box full, and a screen full of missed calls is its own kind of alarming to walk back into.

Send a message as well as calling, because a message waits and a call doesn't. If there's a landline, try it, since a landline rings in the room rather than in whichever pocket or handbag the phone is in. Then call the people around them. The other parent. The person who lives in the house. The aunt they speak to daily. It is much easier to ask a relative "have you heard from Amma today?" than it is to ask a stranger to go and knock.

Decide your threshold before you need it, not during

This is the part almost nobody does, and it's the one that makes the whole thing bearable. Sit down on a normal day and finish this sentence: if I can't reach them for ____ hours, and it's during the day their time, I will call ____.

Write the name and the number in your phone under something you'd recognize at three in the morning. It doesn't have to be an elaborate system. It has to exist before the day you need it, because on that day you will not be thinking clearly, and having decided in advance is the difference between acting and sitting there refreshing your phone for another two hours.

A rough ladder, which you should adjust to your own parent:

  1. Call again, and message. Twenty minutes apart, two or three times.
  2. Call the people in the house or nearby, if there are any.
  3. Ask someone local to physically look. A neighbor, a relative, the building manager or front desk, the housekeeper, a friend who lives close. This is the step that resolves it, one way or the other, faster than anything else.
  4. Call the police non-emergency line and ask for a welfare check. This is what that line is for. You are not wasting anyone's time.
  5. Call emergency services if you have a specific reason to think something has happened right now: a recent fall, a call that cut off, a known heart or breathing condition, a recent discharge from hospital.

Jump straight down the ladder when the facts justify it. If your father fell last week and now isn't answering, that is not an hour-by-hour situation.

What a welfare check actually is

Someone in uniform goes to the address and tries to make contact. They knock. If nobody answers and there's reason to think somebody inside needs help, they may force entry, and they may not. Whether they arrive within the hour or much later depends entirely on where your parent lives and what else is happening that night. It is a real and useful thing to be able to ask for. It is not a guarantee, and it is not a diagnosis. If an officer reports back that everything looked fine through a window, that is one person's glance, not a medical assessment, and you are allowed to keep pushing if you are still worried.

It also has a social cost, and it's worth naming rather than pretending otherwise. Your parent may be embarrassed. They may be angry that you sent someone. Extended family may hear about it and have opinions. My own view, having sat in that hour more than once, is that this cost is real and it is still much smaller than the alternative, and that most parents forgive it a good deal faster than we expect.

Have this ready before you make that call

Whoever you end up calling, they will ask, and having it written down already saves you from trying to remember an address while frightened.

The full address with the apartment number, the gate code, the floor, which door. Your parent's full name and age. Whether they live alone. Any medical conditions, and the medicines they take. Who has a key, or whether the building has one. Whether there's a dog. And the specific reason you're worried today, in plain sentences: not "I'm anxious about my mother", but "she answers every morning by nine, it's two in the afternoon there, she had a fall three weeks ago, and her neighbor hasn't seen her since Tuesday." Specifics get people to the door faster than worry does.

All of it fits on one sheet, and there's a free printable one on this site, the in-case-something-happens sheet, so it's written down before the day you need it rather than remembered during it.

Afterwards, close the gap

If it turned out to be nothing, which it usually does, resist the urge to never speak of it again. That hour told you something true: that between you and your parent's front door there was nobody, and no plan.

Fix the cheap parts of that this week. Get one name and number of somebody who lives close enough to walk over, and ask them first so it's a person who has agreed rather than a person you're about to surprise. Find out whether the building has a master key and who holds it. Make sure the phone charges somewhere they'll see it and that the ringer is loud enough. If they have a landline, keep it. And write down what a normal day looks like while things are steady; there's a one-page sheet for that too. It's what turns "she's not answering" into "she's not answering at a time she always answers", which is the difference between a feeling and a reason.

And if you want a wider look at what else is worth noticing, the okay-living-alone checklist covers thirty things, of which "you often can't reach them and nobody is nearby" is number thirty, and the one people skip.

# My 3 personal stories

## 1. The movie that makes me want to call

Sometimes I’ll watch an emotional movie about the love between a parent and child, an elderly person suffering, or simply a family having fun together. It reminds me of my parents, and I immediately want to call and check on them.

But the timing is usually off. Because of the time difference, it may not be a good time to call them. Or I may be in the middle of something I can’t step away from, with no room for the meaningful, unhurried conversation I want to have. So I don’t call right then.

That doesn’t make the feeling go away. The longer I wait, the more anxious I become, and my brain starts inventing the worst possible things that could have happened to them. With the Kettle app, I can take a quick look and confirm that their routine is happening normally. Then I can call later in the day, when I have the time and attention to really talk.

## 2. The day my dad was mugged

A few years ago, my dad was mugged on the street. A thief riding a bike stole his cell phone and rode away.

That incident freaked me out about my parents’ safety. It was a reminder that something could happen suddenly, during an ordinary day, while I was far away.

## 3. When my mother doesn’t hear the phone

My mother is hard of hearing. When she doesn’t put on her hearing aid, she may not hear the phone at all—even when I call her.

Often, she eventually sees the missed call and calls me back. But by then the time difference may have kicked in, or I may be in the middle of something that prevents me from answering. If I do answer, I’m already panicked, asking whether everything is okay.

With the Kettle app, I’ll be able to see that she is continuing her normal routine and relax. When we do speak, I can call her in the right frame of mind instead of beginning the conversation in a panic.

## 4. Friends' stories

As I'm getting older, I'm hearing more stories from friends about issues with their parents: caring after a fall, caring for them from afar. These have been increasing in frequency as we get older. One guy I knew disappeared for about 2–3 weeks; when I met him playing soccer he mentioned his dad fell and he had to go and arrange round-the-clock caretakers, which took a few weeks to sort out. He's still worried about how they'll take care of his dad if he isn't around.

When you hear stories like this your mind wanders: what would I do in that situation? Just knowing they're active offers a slight sense of relief. I don't want to make this a scary thing, but these are part of the things we tend to discuss.

# Blog post 2 — draft

**Working title:** The button in the drawer

**Alternate 1:** What we're actually asking them to wear

**Alternate 2:** They're not being stubborn

---

If you've bought your mother one of those buttons she's meant to press if she falls, you can probably guess where it is right now. Not around her neck. It's in a drawer, or on the counter next to the mail, or beside the bed still attached to its charger. She wore it for a few days after it arrived, because you'd gone to the trouble, and then it quietly retired. The watch that does the same job tends to end up in the same drawer. So does the camera you put up in the hallway, assuming it didn't get turned to face the wall first.

For a long time I read this as stubbornness. You buy the thing, you explain the thing, they agree the thing is sensible, and then nothing happens, and after the third conversation you start to feel like you're arguing with a wall that gave birth to you. But I've come around to thinking the refusal is more coherent than it looks, and that the mistake is mine, not theirs.

Here's what changed my mind. The same parent who won't touch a cane will happily accept a nice insulated mug, a robot vacuum, a big clock for the wall, a night light in the hall. Not just accept, actually like. That's the same person, the same house, the same money, and in some cases more fiddling about with settings than the cane would ever have required. So whatever's going on isn't about cost, and it isn't about the technology being hard, and it certainly isn't that they don't want their life to be easier.

It's about what the object says about them. A mug says nothing. A clock says nothing, even when you bought it because they'd been mixing up the days. A night light in the hall is invisible by design, it just makes the trip to the bathroom at two in the morning less of an event, and nobody has to agree out loud that the trip to the bathroom at two in the morning is a risk now. But a button worn on the chest is not really an object. It's a sentence, and the sentence is: we think there's a fair chance you'll end up on your own floor unable to get up, and we'd like you dressed for that possibility every day, including on the good days, including in front of your friends. Every single morning it asks them to agree with our assessment of their future. That's a lot to ask of a small piece of plastic.

I've heard the argument that falling is something that already happens and already gets dealt with, so a device for it is an answer to a question nobody asked. From outside that sounds like denial, and sometimes it is. But from inside, every one of those falls ended with them getting themselves up off the floor and carrying on with the day. Their evidence, from where they're standing, is a perfect record. Ours is imagination. We're not arguing about the same thing.

The money objection usually turns up second, by the way, once the first refusal has been pushed on. The monthly fee is real, and on a fixed income it's not nothing, but I've noticed it tends to arrive after the dignity objection rather than before it, and it's easier to say out loud. "It's expensive" is a much more comfortable sentence than "I don't want to be the kind of person who needs one of these."

Then there's the two-week pattern, which is the one that took me longest to accept. Give somebody in their seventies or eighties a new thing to learn, and they will often learn it, out of love or politeness, and use it properly for a couple of weeks. Then it goes back to how it was before. This isn't failure and it isn't laziness. It's that a habit built at eighty on top of a habit built over fifty years doesn't stick, and every day the new thing has to win again. Anything that needs to be charged, remembered, worn, tapped or re-learned is running that race daily, and it loses eventually.

And I should be honest about my own side of this, because I don't think the pendant is only about them. What I want isn't really for my mother to wear a button. What I want is to stop wondering. The button is a way of converting my worry into an object and handing it to her to carry, and when she leaves it in a drawer, what I feel isn't only concern for her safety, it's the return of a feeling I'd hoped to be rid of. That's worth admitting before the next conversation, because they can hear it in your voice. It's also why the argument gets heated so quickly. Nobody's really arguing about a necklace.

The people I've heard about who get further with this tend to do a few things differently. They aren't the one who says it, they let a doctor or a physical therapist be the messenger, because a parent can accept from a professional what they can't accept from their own child without it meaning something about the family. They pitch things as convenient rather than as safe. They put several things in the house at once so there's one awkward afternoon instead of six. And they pay attention to the fact that a house full of small helpful objects that ask nothing goes down much better than one device that asks something every day.

[ANECDOTE — needs: either a time you pushed a suggestion and it went badly, or a moment one of your parents explained their side of it and it landed.]

The other thing worth saying is that the button, even worn perfectly, doesn't stop anybody falling. It shortens how long they're on the floor. That matters enormously, I'm not dismissing it. But when I hear people describe what they actually want, it's usually further upstream than that: better light on the stairs, a bar in the bathroom, the rug that's been curling at the edge for a decade finally gone. Nobody refuses a light bulb.

I built HeyKettle because of this. It asks nothing of them.

None of this means the refusal is easy to live with. You can understand exactly why your father won't wear the thing and still lie awake about it. But I've stopped treating it as a problem with him. He spent sixty years being the person other people relied on. Being asked to wear the proof that he isn't any more, and to do it daily, in his own house, is not a small request, and "no" is not an unreasonable answer to it.

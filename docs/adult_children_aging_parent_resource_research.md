# Resource Research: Adult Children Monitoring Aging Parents

**Research focus:** Adult children helping aging parents remain safely independent, especially when the child does not live with the parent.

**Research date:** August 28, 2026

## Strategic takeaway

The strongest content opportunity is not generic "senior printables." It is a focused library that helps an adult child answer:

- Is Mom or Dad still doing okay living alone?
- What changes should I watch for?
- What is "normal" for my parent?
- When should I call, visit, or get additional help?
- How can siblings coordinate without constantly checking in?
- What should I have ready before an emergency?
- How can I help without taking away independence?

A strong positioning theme is:

> **Know what normal looks like. Notice when it changes.**

That naturally connects educational content to passive monitoring.

# 1. Monitoring, Independence, and "Does My Parent Need Help?"

## 1. Aging Parent Needs-Help Checklist
**Source:** National Institute on Aging (NIA) — "Does an Older Adult in Your Life Need Help?"

Create a printable for adult children covering observable changes in:

- Food and meal preparation
- Hygiene
- Household condition
- Medication routines
- Weight
- Mobility
- Falls
- Confusion
- Social isolation
- Memory and decision-making

**Content opportunity:** "15 Signs Your Aging Parent May Need More Help at Home"

**Fit for passive monitoring:** Excellent.

Source:
https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/caregiving/does-older-adult-your-life-need-help

## 2. "What Is Normal for Mom or Dad?" Baseline Worksheet
Inspired by the NIA guidance on recognizing changes.

Have families record the parent's usual:

- Wake-up time
- Bedtime
- Meal schedule
- Typical movement around the house
- Outings
- Medication routine
- Phone/contact habits
- Household tasks
- Social activities

**Fit for passive monitoring:** Excellent. This directly explains why changes from baseline can matter.

## 3. Changes I've Noticed Log
A dated observation log for adult children and siblings.

Columns:

- Date
- What changed
- How unusual was it?
- Who noticed it?
- Did we contact Mom/Dad?
- Follow-up required
- Notes

**Fit:** Excellent.

## 4. Independent Living Check-In Scorecard
A simple monthly family review covering:

- Mobility
- Meals
- Medication management
- Personal care
- Household upkeep
- Transportation
- Communication
- Social activity
- Safety
- Finances

**Fit:** Excellent.

## 5. "When Should I Check on Mom?" Decision Guide
A family-friendly escalation sheet:

- Normal variation
- Worth a phone call
- Worth asking a neighbor
- Worth visiting
- Contact a health professional
- Emergency situation

Avoid medical diagnosis; frame it as a communication and safety-planning tool.

**Fit:** Excellent.

# Particularly Strong Positioning for a Passive Monitoring Service

Avoid positioning the service as:

> Watch your elderly parent.

A stronger independence-preserving message is:

> **Stay aware without hovering.**

Or:

> **Know when something changes — without putting a camera in the home.**

Or:

> **Help Mom or Dad stay independent while giving the family greater awareness.**

The educational concept tying your downloads together should be:

> **Know what normal looks like. Notice when it changes.**

This makes your educational content and product proposition reinforce one another.

# Reuse / Copyright Notes

## Federal government resources

Many works created by U.S. federal government employees as part of their official duties are not subject to U.S. copyright protection. However:

- Do not assume every item on a `.gov` website is government-authored.
- Third-party photographs, illustrations, partner content, and licensed materials may have separate rights.
- Agency names, seals, and logos should not be used in a way that suggests endorsement.
- Check the specific resource before reproducing it.

NIA specifically states that its caregiver and doctor worksheets can be downloaded, printed, and copied.

CDC Stacks identifies some CDC publications, including versions of "Check for Safety," as public domain.

## CFPB

CFPB is particularly useful for outreach content because it provides free educational materials and explicitly offers co-branding options for some "Managing Someone Else's Money" guides.

## Nonprofit resources

For resources from organizations such as:

- Alzheimer's Association
- Family Caregiver Alliance
- American Red Cross
- National Council on Aging

Treat them primarily as:

- research
- inspiration
- authoritative references
- link-out resources

unless the organization provides explicit reuse/republication permission.

Do not simply copy their PDF, remove their branding, and publish it as your own.

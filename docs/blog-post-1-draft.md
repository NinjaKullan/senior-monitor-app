# Blog post 1 — draft (v4)

**Working title:** The call I've rehearsed and never made

**Alternate 1:** When my mother doesn't hear the phone

**Alternate 2:** Who would I even call?

---

My parents live in Chennai and I live in the US, which means that most of the time I'm awake, they're asleep, and the window when we can actually talk is a couple of hours a day if everyone's schedule cooperates.

My mother is hard of hearing. When she doesn't have her hearing aid in, she won't hear the phone ring. So I'll call and get nothing, and call again and get nothing. Usually she notices the missed calls later and calls me back, but by then it's often a bad time on my end, I'm in a meeting or driving or in the middle of something, and I see her name come up and can't answer. When we do finally connect, the first thing out of my mouth isn't hello. It's "is everything okay?" She's fine. She was fine the whole time. But I've spent the last few hours quietly assuming otherwise, and now the one conversation we'll have that day starts with me sounding worried and her wondering why.

I think anyone with a parent living far away knows the version of this that happens in your head. You call, no answer. You tell yourself it's nothing, they're probably out. An hour goes by. You start checking: when did I last actually hear her voice, not just a message? Does she normally pick up at this time? Did Dad say they were going anywhere? Is the phone even charged?

Most of the time this ends with a call back and a slightly puzzled "why do you sound like that?" and you let it go. But there's a point, somewhere in those hours, where it stops being something you can let go. Once you're past it, you have to do something. And the something is uncomfortable to even think about, because it means calling another person and asking them to go and knock on your parents' door.

It might be a neighbour you've met twice. It might be the watchman at their building, or a cousin who lives forty minutes away. Whoever it is, you're going to ring them and say, as calmly as you can, "I can't reach my mother, could you go and check on her?" And the reason it's so hard is that the moment you make that call, you've said out loud that you're scared of something real. Up to then it could still be nothing. Now it's a welfare check, and someone is going to walk up to that door not knowing what they'll find, and neither do you.

There's a second fear stacked on top of the first one, which is the fear of being wrong. If the neighbour goes over and my father opens the door, perfectly fine, annoyed at the interruption, then I've made a scene. He'll hear about it. He'll ask why I'm sending people round to his house, and he'll have a point. And he'll be a little more careful about what he tells me next time, which is exactly what I don't want. So you wait a bit longer. Then a bit longer than that. And each extra hour you wait is an hour that would matter, if this turned out to be the time it wasn't nothing.

Call too early and you're the child who hovers. Call too late and you're the child who waited. I haven't found a version of this where you feel good afterwards.

I wasn't always this jumpy about it. A few years ago my dad was out walking in Chennai and a thief on a bike rode past and took his phone. He was okay. As these things go it was minor, and he was more irritated than shaken. But it stayed with me for a long time, mostly because of how ordinary it was. He was doing something he does every day, and in a couple of seconds something happened to him that I had no way of knowing about, from where I was, until someone told me. I couldn't have done anything about it. I understood that. I just didn't like what it implied about every other ordinary day.

It isn't only my own parents, either. As I've got older, the conversations with friends have changed. Someone's mother had a fall. Someone else is trying to organise care from the other side of the world and it's eating their weekends. Someone I know dropped out of sight for a few weeks, and when I ran into him again he told me his dad had fallen and he'd had to go and arrange round-the-clock caretakers, and it had taken him most of that time to get it sorted. He'd managed it. But he was still worried about how they'd look after his father the next time, when he wasn't there. I don't want to make this sound grim, because it isn't, mostly. It's just what we talk about now, in between everything else. And every time you hear one of these, your mind wanders off to your own parents and what you'd do.

So, like a lot of people, I've made quiet plans without ever calling them plans. I know which neighbour's number I'd use. I know which cousin actually picks up. I've thought about who has a spare key. None of it is dramatic. What wears on you is carrying it around every day in case you need it.

And what's strange is how little it takes to set the worry off. It's rarely anything real. Sometimes it's a film. I'll watch something about a parent and a grown child, or an old man alone in a house, or just a family eating dinner together, and I'll suddenly want to call my parents. But it's the middle of the night in Chennai, or I'm in the middle of something I can't put down, and the conversation I'm in the mood for is the long, unhurried kind that won't fit into couple of minutes I actually have. So I don't call. And the wanting to call doesn't fade. It turns into wondering whether they're okay, and within an hour I've built up a fairly detailed picture of everything that could have gone wrong since we last spoke.

What I eventually realised is that almost none of this worry comes from anything that's happened. It comes from not knowing whether today is a normal day. If I knew that my mother was up and about at 7 in the morning her time, the way she always is, most of this would never get started. A missed call at 3 in the afternoon would just be a missed call. The film would just be a film. The point at which I'd have to ring a neighbour would be much further away, because I'd already know the one thing I actually want to know, which is that today looks like every other day. And when I did call, later, with time, I could start with hello.

I built HeyKettle because of this. It sends me a short note in the morning and another in the evening saying that my parents' day looks normal, and it asks nothing of them. They don't have to wear anything, charge anything, tap anything, or learn anything.

I don't know if I'll ever have to make that other call. I hope not. But I'd rather spend my evenings knowing the day started than rehearsing what I'd say to the neighbour.

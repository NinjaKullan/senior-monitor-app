# Kettle — go-to-market research

**Date:** August 23, 2026 · **Scope:** US market · **Method:** live web research (five parallel research passes, ~140 sources; key facts and sampled quotes independently re-verified). Every number and quote carries a link. Quotes are verbatim retrieved text. Where sources conflict, both are shown. UNKNOWN means it could not be sourced — nothing here is estimated silently.

Kettle for reference: $10/month; notices whether the parent's ordinary phone routine happened; calm note to the adult child twice a day; quiet morning → asks the parent first; family only if she doesn't answer. No camera, no wearable, no location, nothing installed.

### 1c. Cameras (what frugal families actually do)

[Ring](https://www.security.org/security-cameras/ring/) ($4.99–$19.99/mo + $60–$230 cam), [Nest/Google](https://store.google.com/product/nest_aware) ($10–$20/mo), [Wyze](https://www.wyze.com/pages/compare) ($2.99–$19.99/mo + ~$20–50 cams). Parent does nothing — but is *watched*. The complaint pattern is trust and dignity, not function: FTC [charged Ring over employee access to customer video](https://tagteam.harvard.edu), Wyze [exposed 13,000 customers' camera feeds to strangers](https://topclassactions.com/lawsuit-settlements/privacy/data-breach/wyze-data-breach-allowed-13000-customers-to-view-other-peoples-homes/), and family forums are full of guilt threads about spying (§5d).

### 1e. App trackers

[Life360](https://www.life360.com/plans-pricing) (free–$24.99/mo): parent must carry a phone with location always on; the recurring theme in coverage/reviews is battery drain and the surveillance feel [snippet]. Built for teens, adopted awkwardly for elders.

### 1f. Passive phone-routine products — Kettle's actual lane

Search across app stores and the open web surfaced only one clear US analog: **[Parents Are OK](https://parentsareok.app)** — installs on the parent's phone, passively watches screen touches/app use, alerts family after ~24h of silence; pricing unpublished, no visible review base or distribution. A handful of unvetted app-store check-in apps exist ([I'm OK: Safety Check](https://apps.apple.com/vn/app/im-ok-safety-check/id6757763851), [Are You Ok?](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/-/id6759046980)). **The lane is essentially empty of funded, marketed competitors.**

### 1g. Amazon — the giant that entered and left

- **Alexa Together**: launched [Dec 7, 2021 at $19.99/mo](https://techcrunch.com/2021/12/07/amazon-launches-its-19-99-per-month-alexa-together-elder-care-subscription-for-families/) — remote assist, Urgent Response, and a **passive activity feed** ("see your parent was up and moving without calling"). Amazon retired it — [May 2025 per secondary coverage](https://besidecare.com/blog/what-to-use-now-that-alexa-together-is-gone/); Amazon's own product pages now 404, and no official announcement remains findable, so treat the exact date as unofficial. The shutdown left families searching for replacements.
- **[Alexa Emergency Assist](https://www.reviewed.com/accessibility/content/alexa-emergency-assist-review)** ($5.99/mo Prime, $7.99 non-Prime) replaced only the emergency-calling piece. **The passive activity feed — the most Kettle-like feature on the mainstream market — was dropped, not replaced.**

## 2. Market size

**The parent side (who Kettle watches):**

- **16.2 million** community-dwelling adults 65+ lived alone in 2023 — 28% of the 65+ population; 10.5M women (33% of older women), 5.7M men (22%) — [ACL/Census, 2023 Profile of Older Americans](https://acl.gov/sites/default/files/Profile%20of%20OA/ACL_ProfileOlderAmericans2023_508.pdf). Pew's decennial-census read puts the share slightly lower at [26% (Dec 2025)](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/12/04/a-smaller-share-of-older-us-adults-live-alone-today-than-in-1990/) — either way, ~15–16M people.
- Living alone concentrates exactly where Kettle's story does: [43% of women 75+ live alone](https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2024/05/living-arrangements.html) (Census 2024); 38% of adults 85+ ([Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/12/04/a-smaller-share-of-older-us-adults-live-alone-today-than-in-1990/)).
- ~80–85% of adults 70+ have had children ([Pew 2024: 15% of 70+ never had children](https://www.pewresearch.org/social-trends/2024/07/25/demographic-and-economic-characteristics-of-adults-50-and-older-without-children/)) → **roughly 13M parents 65+ living alone with living adult children.**
- Phone reachability is no longer the constraint: **78% of adults 65+ own a smartphone** ([Pew, Jan 2026](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/01/08/internet-use-smartphone-ownership-digital-divides-in-u-s/)), up from 61% in 2021.

**The child side (who pays):**

- **63 million Americans — 1 in 4 adults — provided family care in the past year**, up 45% in a decade ([AARP/NAC, Caregiving in the US 2025](https://www.aarp.org/pri/topics/ltss/family-caregiving/caregiving-in-the-us-2025/)). **40% care for a parent or parent-in-law** ([report PDF](https://caregivingkinetics.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/AARP-NAC-caregiving-in-us-2025.doi_.10.26419-2fppi.00373.001.pdf)) → **~25M adults caring for a parent right now** — and Kettle's buyer is arguably upstream of even this ("worried, not yet caregiving").
- **23% of all US adults are sandwiched** — living parent 65+ AND raising/supporting a child ([Pew 2022](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2022/04/08/more-than-half-of-americans-in-their-40s-are-sandwiched-between-an-aging-parent-and-their-own-children/)); among Americans in their 40s it's 54%.
- Caregivers already spend real money: **$7,242/yr average out-of-pocket**, 78% incur regular costs ([AARP 2021](https://www.aarp.org/pri/topics/ltss/family-caregiving/family-caregivers-cost-survey/) — still the latest published figure). Kettle at $120/yr = **1.7% of the existing average spend**.
- ~26% of caregivers live 20+ minutes from the recipient; 12% an hour or more ([AARP/NAC 2025](https://www.aarp.org/pri/topics/ltss/family-caregiving/caregiving-in-the-us-2025/)) — the distance segment that can't "just drop by."

**Who pays for monitoring tech today (the honest, small number):**

- Only **~9% of adults 65+ use a medical-alert device** ([The Senior List consumer study, 2026, n=909](https://www.theseniorlist.com/research/medical-alert-device-consumer-usage-study/); [U-Michigan National Poll on Healthy Aging](https://www.consumeraffairs.com/medical-alert-systems/medical-alert-statistics.html) agrees: <10%).
- US PERS/medical-alert market revenue: **$2.9B (2025)** per [Towards Healthcare](https://www.towardshealthcare.com/insights/us-medical-alert-systems-market-sizing); [Grand View](https://www.grandviewresearch.com/industry-analysis/medical-alert-personal-emergency-response-system-pers-market) had $4.1B (2023) — analyst figures conflict, but the shape is "single-digit billions, growing ~10%/yr."
- Yet **46% of adults 50+ own broader home-safety tech** (alarms, doorbell/driveway cameras — [AARP 2025 Tech Trends](https://www.aarp.org/pri/topics/technology/internet-media-devices/2025-technology-trends-older-adults/)). Families buy *safety*; they resist *medical devices*. The budget exists under a different mental category.
- Only **~8% of family caregivers report spending anything on remote monitoring** ([AARP survey via MobiHealthNews](https://www.mobihealthnews.com/content/aarp-survey-finds-just-8-percent-family-caregivers-spend-money-remote-monitoring-devices)).

**Back-of-envelope SAM:** 13M parents 65+ living alone with adult children × $120/yr ≈ **$1.6B/yr** at full penetration of the narrowest definition — before counting couples, 60–64s, or multiple paying siblings.

## 3. Pricing — where $10 sits

The market's monthly price ladder ([sources in §1](#1-competitive-landscape--what-an-adult-child-buys-instead)):

| Band | Who's there | Parent's burden |
|---|---|---|
| **$0** | Snug free tier; police "Are You OK?" call programs; Alexa Drop-In; the child's own daily call | Daily tap / scheduled answer / sibling labor |
| **$5–15** | Alexa Emergency Assist $5.99; Walabot $4.99 (after $150/room hardware); Iamfine $10–14.99; Snug Dispatch $12.50; **Kettle $10** | Every one of these except Kettle requires the parent to *do* something (tap, answer, own an Echo) or mount hardware |
| **$25–45** | Mainstream PERS (Medical Guardian, Bay Alarm, MobileHelp, ADT, GetSafe, LifeFone) | Wear a pendant 24/7 |
| **$40–70** | Life Alert base; Aloe Care; Sensi (agency-priced) | Wearing + installed hub/sensors |
| **$70–100+** | CarePredict $69.99 + $499; EnvoyAtHome $99 + $399; Life Alert full stack ~$110 | Wearables + beacons, or full sensor install |

What the data says about $10:

- **Stated willingness-to-pay is ~5x higher than Kettle's price**: caregivers willing to pay averaged **~$50/month for monitoring-only technology** (Schulz et al., *The Gerontologist*, [n=512](https://academic.oup.com/gerontologist/article/56/5/817/2605281)); ~20% would pay nothing.
- The real risk at $10 is not "too cheap to trust" — it's invisible margin: nothing is left for paid acquisition (see §7). No evidence was found that low price *itself* deters purchase in this category (price-as-trust-signal research exists only outside eldercare — [UNKNOWN for this market](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11002-025-09769-3)).
- **The trust objection incumbents have earned is the opposite one**: overpaying and being trapped. Life Alert's 3-year non-cancelable contract, $95–$245 activation, and a BBB file of [cancellation-after-death horror stories](https://www.complaintsboard.com/life-alert-emergency-response-b123256) (charged $240 after confirmed cancellation; death certificate "not received"; $300 drafted three months post-cancel). **$10/month, cancel anytime, no hardware** is a positioning weapon aimed at a documented wound.
- Churn/tenure benchmarks: UNKNOWN — no published PERS retention data exists (the industry association [rebranded in 2025](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/medical-alert-monitoring-association-rebrands-as-connected-health--safety-association-302559442.html) without publishing any). Assume tenure is capped by the parent's trajectory (decline → facility → death), i.e., LTV is structurally finite for everyone in this market.

**Read:** $10 is a wedge, not a credibility problem — *provided* the marketing never claims to be an emergency-response service (that's what $30+ buys: 24/7 human dispatch). Kettle sells a different thing: the daily answer to "is she okay today?" Priced like a streaming service, not like a medical device — which matches how 46% of 50+ households already buy "home safety."

## 6. The five sharpest facts for positioning

1. **16.2M Americans 65+ live alone; ~9% use any medical-alert device.** ([ACL/Census 2023](https://acl.gov/sites/default/files/Profile%20of%20OA/ACL_ProfileOlderAmericans2023_508.pdf); [Senior List 2026](https://www.theseniorlist.com/research/medical-alert-device-consumer-usage-study/)) The market isn't crowded — it's 90% unserved, because everything on sale demands the parent wear, install, tap, or answer something.

2. **The incumbent category fails at its own job, in writing:** *"My mother fell and laid on the floor for 2 days with the life alert button around her neck."* ([BBB, May 2026](https://www.bbb.org/us/ca/encino/profile/medical-alarm/life-alert-emergency-response-inc-1216-23351/complaints)) Every review theme — won't wear it, false alarms, 3-year contracts, billed after death — is a Kettle differentiator by design.

3. **The buyer already spends $7,242/yr out of pocket on this parent** ([AARP](https://www.aarp.org/pri/topics/ltss/family-caregiving/family-caregivers-cost-survey/)), and caregivers who'd pay for monitoring quote **~$50/mo willingness** ([The Gerontologist](https://academic.oup.com/gerontologist/article/56/5/817/2605281)). $10 is not a price objection; it's an impulse-subscribe. The constraint is belief, not budget.

4. **Amazon proved the demand and then abandoned the lane.** Alexa Together ($19.99/mo, [launched Dec 2021](https://techcrunch.com/2021/12/07/amazon-launches-its-19-99-per-month-alexa-together-elder-care-subscription-for-families/), retired [~May 2025](https://besidecare.com/blog/what-to-use-now-that-alexa-together-is-gone/)) had exactly one Kettle-like feature — the passive "she's up and moving" feed — and its replacement [dropped it](https://besidecare.com/blog/what-to-use-now-that-alexa-together-is-gone/). No funded, marketed, zero-install passive product exists in the US today (§1f). First-mover language is honestly available.

5. **63M caregivers is the fastest-growing big market in the country** — up 45% in ten years, 1 in 4 US adults, 40% caring for a parent ([AARP/NAC 2025](https://www.aarp.org/pri/topics/ltss/family-caregiving/caregiving-in-the-us-2025/)) — and 78% of their parents now carry smartphones ([Pew 2026](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/01/08/internet-use-smartphone-ownership-digital-divides-in-u-s/)). The "phone routine" substrate Kettle reads is newly universal; this product wasn't buildable in 2019.

## 7. Three channels for the ~20-family paid beta

1. **WhatsApp-native diaspora families, starting with your own network.** Kettle's notes arrive on WhatsApp — 32% of US adults use it, but **45% of Hispanic and 39% of Asian-American adults** do ([Pew, Nov 2025](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2025/11/20/americans-social-media-use-2025/)), and immigrant families already run on the daily-call ritual Kettle automates. Founder-family beta is literally this segment; recruit outward through community WhatsApp groups, cultural associations, temple/church networks. Expect the first 8–10 families here at near-zero CAC — and the most forgiving early users.

2. **Facebook caregiver groups + the "Working Daughter" micro-media ecosystem, via the front door.** DM admins of Working Daughter, The Caregiver Space, Parenting Aging Parents-type groups with a founder-story post offer plus free beta accounts for the group; pitch Liz O'Donnell and Daughterhood directly; book the [Happy Healthy Caregiver media kit](https://happyhealthycaregiver.com/podcast/) if paid help is needed. These audiences are precisely employed adult daughters — the documented majority buyer. Reddit is explicitly hostile (permanent-ban rules) — use it to listen, plus at most one mod-approved r/CaregiverSupport "Research Wednesday" post.

3. **Geriatric care managers (ALCA) as a referral seed.** [2,000+ members, formal vendor program](https://www.aginglifecare.org/about/). Three to five GCM relationships ("my client's mother isn't ready for a PERS — try this $10 thing and I'll look better for suggesting it") can fill the remaining slots with high-intent families and produce professional testimonials for launch. Slower to start; compounding.

*(Deliberately not on the list: paid social. At $120/yr revenue, PERS-style CACs would be underwater — see §8.)*

## 8. What contradicts the premise

- **Fear does not convert to wallet at rest.** Only ~8% of caregivers spend anything on remote monitoring ([AARP](https://www.mobihealthnews.com/content/aarp-survey-finds-just-8-percent-family-caregivers-spend-money-remote-monitoring-devices)); ~20% would pay nothing at all ([Gerontologist](https://academic.oup.com/gerontologist/article/56/5/817/2605281)). And the VoC is unambiguous: **people buy after the fall, not before it** (§5e). Kettle is a pre-crisis product in a post-crisis-purchase market. GTM must intercept near-misses — "she didn't answer for two days" — not sell prevention in the abstract.
- **Amazon's exit cuts both ways.** The generous read (fact 4) is "lane vacated." The harsh read: Amazon, with free distribution into millions of homes and a $19.99 price, couldn't make the economics work. The difference to bet on: Alexa Together required an Echo, setup, and Amazon trust; Kettle requires nothing in the home. But treat the exit as evidence the category is *hard*, not just open.
- **"Ordinary phone routine" is a noisy signal for some parents.** 78% of 65+ own smartphones, but only 14% are heavy ("almost constant") users ([Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/01/08/internet-use-smartphone-ownership-digital-divides-in-u-s/)); light or erratic users will generate false quiet mornings. False alarms are the single most-documented churn driver in this entire market (§5f). If Kettle's quiet-morning rate isn't tuned per-parent, it inherits the exact failure it's positioned against. The dark-run data you're collecting now is the moat — treat it that way.
- **The real competitor is free.** The child's own daily call, a sibling rota, Alexa Drop-In, and police reassurance programs all cost $0. Kettle's pitch must be honest about what $10 replaces: not the call (keep calling!), but the burden of *never being able to miss one* — vacations, meetings, timezones, the 19-day silence thread in §5a.
- **"No camera" doesn't fully dissolve the surveillance objection.** The dignity language in §5c–d attaches to being watched *at all*. Phone-routine observation is the gentlest tier that exists, but the parent-consent conversation is still Kettle's to design for — "it asks her first" is the feature to lead with, because it makes the parent the first respondent, not the subject.
- **A direct analog already exists** ([Parents Are OK](https://parentsareok.app), passive phone-activity → family alert), plus Snug at $12.50/mo with a free tier and years of App Store reviews. Neither has visible funding or marketing — but "nobody has ever tried this" is not available as a claim, and Snug's free tier caps what "check-in" alone can charge. Kettle's defensible claims are *passive*, *zero-install*, *asks her first*, and *calm by default* — not price and not novelty.

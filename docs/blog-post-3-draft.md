# Blog post 3 — draft

**Working title:** Small things that actually help

**Alternate 1:** The stuff that doesn't sit in the hallway

**Alternate 2:** Cheap, boring, and used every day

---

When you start worrying about a parent living on their own, the instinct is to go big. Move them. Hire someone. Buy the expensive thing. All of that has its place, but there's a layer underneath it that gets skipped, and it's the layer where most of the actual day happens. Getting out of bed. Getting into the shower. Picking up the thing you dropped. Working out what day it is.

The pattern I keep noticing is that the small stuff gets used and the big stuff sits in the hallway. A house can contain three unused walkers and two canes and still not have a decent light on the landing. It's not random which things get adopted. Anything that reads as equipment for an old person gets refused, sometimes for years. Anything that reads as a nice object, or a convenience, or is simply invisible once installed, goes in without a fight. Same parent, same house, same money.

So here's the layer underneath, roughly in order of how little argument it causes.

The ones nobody objects to:

- **Reachers, and more than one.** The long-handled grabber for picking things up off the floor. Buy three or four and leave one in each room, because the point is that it's within reach of wherever the thing gets dropped. It reads as a tool rather than a medical device, which is why it's the rare aid that gets used from the first day.

- **Plug-in night lights that come on by themselves.** The route from the bed to the bathroom in the middle of the night is where a lot goes wrong, and the reason people make that trip in the dark is that they don't want to wake anyone by turning on the hall light. A light that handles itself removes the choice. Install them and never mention them again.

- **A clock that shows the day and date in big letters.** This is the one I've seen recommended more than anything else, by a mile. If someone's started mixing up Tuesday and Wednesday, this fixes the actual problem, which is not memory but not having anywhere to check. It has a way of spreading through a parent's circle of friends, which tells you something about how it feels to own one.

- **A good insulated mug.** Tea going cold because it took twenty minutes to get across the room, and drinks getting knocked over, come up constantly and never get solved. A heavy mug with a lid deals with both. It also reads as a present rather than an aid, which matters more than it should.

- **Something to keep the phone attached to them.** A lanyard, a strap, a grip on the back. Dropped phones are a real problem with a weaker grip, and a cracked screen turns into a week of not being able to reach each other.

- **A magnifier with a light in it.** Small print on medicine labels, letters from the bank, instructions on a packet. The lit ones beat the plain ones, because the problem is usually as much light as magnification.

- **A robot vacuum.** This one surprised me, but it comes up over and over as the most popular thing anyone has given their parents. Cleaning floors is physically hard and it's the chore people quietly give up on first. Don't buy the cheapest one, they get stuck and end up as another abandoned object, and expect to do the setting up yourself on a visit.

- **A doorbell with a camera in it.** Framed as knowing who's at the door before deciding whether to get up, this goes down easily, and given how much unwanted attention arrives at the doors of people living alone, it earns its place on that alone.

- **A shower head on a hose.** Being able to wash while sitting, or without turning under the water, makes the whole business less precarious. It costs little and carries no stigma, so there's no reason to wait.

- **A non-slip mat in the bath, and fewer loose rugs on the floor.** Loose rugs are the single most-named hazard in a home. The mat is easy. The rugs are harder, because they're usually the ones somebody chose, so that needs an actual conversation rather than a quiet swap while they're out.

The ones worth the awkward conversation:

- **A stool or bench for the shower, ideally one that turns.** The strongest language I've come across about any of this is about shower chairs. Getting in and out of a tub is where the fear lives, and sitting down for the whole thing changes it. Expect an initial complaint about how much you spent.

- **Grab bars, properly fixed to the wall.** Not the suction-cup ones. This is the standard advice for a reason, and the bathroom is where it counts. The resistance here is usually about what it looks like to visitors, which is worth taking seriously rather than arguing with.

- **A bidet seat.** People recommend this as a dignity thing, not a gadget. It's the difference between managing alone and needing another person in the room, which is a bigger deal to them than to anyone else.

- **A bar that slips under the mattress.** Something to hold on to while getting in and out of bed, one of those transitions nobody thinks about until it becomes the hard part of the day. It fits under the mattress rather than being screwed to anything.

- **The car kit.** Getting into a car is a hard problem almost nobody writes about. A slippery sheet on the seat to slide across, a handle that fits into the door frame to push up from, and a cushion that turns so the legs come around together. All cheap, all under-known, and it's the difference between going out and not bothering with it.

- **A stove that turns itself off, or a portable induction ring.** The gas left on with the flame out is the biggest kitchen worry there is, and not one you can solve by reminding somebody. Some families skip the device and disconnect the stove instead, which is drastic but honest.

- **Sealed smoke and carbon monoxide detectors with ten-year batteries.** Because the alternative is somebody up a ladder at midnight dealing with a chirping battery, which is one of the more avoidable ways to end up on the floor.

Things I'd leave off: anything that needs charging, anything that needs learning, and anything that has to be worn to work. Not because they're bad, but because they're running an uphill race every day, and after a couple of weeks they lose it.

The bit that took me a while to understand is that none of this is really about the objects. Every one of these is a small negotiation about who somebody is now, and the ones here win because they don't force that conversation. A clock is just a clock. A mat is just a mat. Nobody has to agree out loud that anything has changed, and the house gets a little easier to live in anyway. I built HeyKettle for the same reason: it asks nothing of them.

**Working title:** How often should you check on a parent who lives alone?

**Alternate 1:** There's no right number of calls

Title tag: How often should you check on an elderly parent? · HeyKettle
Meta description: There's no magic number of calls. What matters is a rhythm

---

If you came here for a number, I'll be honest: there isn't one, and the
places that give you one are guessing. I've seen every answer from twice a
day to once a week defended with complete confidence. The families doing
well aren't the ones calling most. They're the ones whose contact has a
shape, so that a break in the shape actually tells them something.

That's the whole principle, and it's worth saying plainly before the
practical part. A call schedule isn't for your parent. Your parent got
through several decades without scheduled calls. It's for you, and what it
buys you isn't reassurance on the days you call. It's meaning on the days
something breaks the pattern. If you talk every morning, one silent morning
says something. If you talk whenever you both get around to it, a silent
week says nothing at all, and that's exactly when the worrying is worst.

## Start from your parent, not from a rule

The right rhythm depends on things only you know. How steady is their
health, and has anything changed lately: a fall, a new diagnosis, a
hospital stay, a new medicine. Whether anyone else lays eyes on them: a
spouse, a neighbor who drops by, a housekeeper, the man who delivers
groceries and would notice an unanswered door. How far away you are, and
how long it would take someone you trust to physically get there. And
honestly, how much contact your parent wants. Some parents would happily
talk twice a day. Some feel checked up on at twice a week, and a parent who
feels checked up on starts telling you less, which defeats the whole point.

A rough shape that fits many families: one real conversation at a
predictable time, a few times a week or daily, plus something lighter on
the other days, a message, a photo, a two-minute hello. After a fall or a
hospital stay, tighten it for a while and say so out loud: "I'm going to
call every morning for a few weeks until you're steady." Parents accept a
temporary change tied to a reason far more easily than a new regime that
arrives without one.

## Make the calls worth answering

Frequency is the wrong thing to optimize. If every call opens with "did you
eat, did you take your pills, did you lock the door," you've turned
yourself into an inspector, and the calls will get shorter and the answers
will get vaguer. The information you actually want, whether they sound like
themselves, comes out of everyday conversation about the neighbor's
renovation and the cricket score, not out of a checklist delivered twice
as often.

So agree the rhythm with them, not for them. "I worry, you know I do.
What suits you?" gets you a schedule they'll actually keep, and a parent
who keeps the schedule is the entire mechanism working.

## Decide now what a broken pattern means

This is the part most families skip, and it's the part that matters. A
rhythm only helps if you've decided what to do when it breaks. If she
answers every morning by nine and it's two in the afternoon, what happens?
Who do you call first? How long do you wait?

Two things make that day survivable. Knowing what their normal actually
looks like, written down while things are steady, so you're comparing
against facts instead of fear. There's [a one-page sheet for
that](/resources/normal-day/). And having your next move decided in
advance, which is its own subject: [what to do when a parent doesn't
answer the phone](/blog/parent-doesnt-answer-the-phone/) walks through it,
from the boring explanations to the welfare check.

## When the answer is "more than a call can carry"

Sometimes the honest answer to "how often should I check?" is that calls no
longer cover it: after repeated falls, when the stove's been left on, when
the weight is coming off. More calls won't fix any of those. That's the
point to look at the [thirty-question checklist](/resources/okay-living-alone/)
and have the bigger conversation, with them and with their doctor, about
what support the week actually needs.

I built HeyKettle because I wanted the days between calls to stop being
blank. It sends me a short note each morning and evening that my parents'
day looks normal, and it asks nothing of them. The calls stayed; they just
went back to being conversations.
